# After the order: what it does, and what it does not do

Read this before you decide whether the order is worth pursuing, and again after it issues.

## Sealed, not erased

An order of nondisclosure **seals** your record. It does not destroy it and it does not erase it.

That is a different thing from an expunction. An expunction destroys records. Nondisclosure hides them from the public and from most private requesters — and the record itself keeps existing.

## Who can still see it

The record stays available to the criminal justice and noncriminal justice agencies the statute lists. That list includes, among others:

- School districts.
- Charter schools and private schools.
- Education service centres.
- Commercial transportation companies.

And here is the part that matters most: **in those contexts, you may not deny the matter.** If one of the listed agencies asks, the honest answer is not "that never happened".

Everywhere else — ordinary private background checks, most employers, most landlords — the sealed record does not show.

## One order covers one offence

An order of nondisclosure applies to **one offence**.

If you have more than one offence you want sealed, you need more than one order. That means more of everything: more papers, more work, and — on the petition route — more filing fees, one per offence.

Know that number before you pay for anything.

## Keep your copy

Keep a copy of the signed order. It is the document that proves the record is sealed, and you may need to show it.

## Stop and talk to a lawyer if

- An agency you did not expect asks you about the matter.
- You are unsure whether a particular employer, licence application or form is one where you may not deny it.
- You are not a United States citizen. Sealing is not erasure, and immigration consequences do not follow the same rules.
- You have several offences and are trying to work out which to pursue.
