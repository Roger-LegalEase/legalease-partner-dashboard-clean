# How this filing goes, start to finish

This page walks you through the order of the steps. It is for you — it is not one of the documents you file.

## One conviction, one complete set

A separate form set is required for each conviction, even if the convictions happened at the same time. Do not combine them into one petition.

## The court stage

1. **Get your records first.** Get your Montana Department of Justice criminal record, and get the judgment and sentencing order from the court that sentenced you. Check your case number and conviction date against them.
2. **Fill in the form set.** That is the petition form for someone currently serving a sentence, the proposed order, and the certificate of service.
3. **Choose what you are asking for.** The form makes you choose between expungement and resentencing. That choice is yours. We do not make it for you, and we do not tell you which is better — they are different outcomes with different consequences.
4. **Write your own explanation.** The form has a free-text space for why the conviction is eligible. Those are your words, not ours.
5. **Sign and date.** You sign your own petition. The court will not consider an unsigned petition. Nothing on the face of the form calls for a notary.
6. **Check the local rules before you file.** You must follow all applicable filing rules, including local filing rules. Montana courts vary. Use the Montana Court Locator to find the local rules and contact information for your court, and ask the clerk if anything is unclear.
7. **File in the court that sentenced you** — the district court, justice court, or municipal or city court that handled the case. File the petition, the proposed order, and the certificate of service together.
8. **Pay the filing fee, or ask for a waiver.** The fee depends on which level of court you are in, so ask the clerk what it is. If you cannot pay, the court instructions point you to the Statement of Inability to Pay Court Costs and Fees on montanalawhelp.org. We link you to that form; we do not fill it in or include it.
9. **Serve the county attorney** for the county you filed in, and complete the certificate of service to show you did. Under the court instructions the county attorney then has 21 days to oppose the petition.
10. **Wait for the court.** The county attorney may respond. The court may set a hearing. We cannot tell you what will happen or when.

## The Department of Justice stage — only after an expungement order

A court order is not the end of this in Montana. If the court **expunges** the conviction, you have one more step, and it is yours to do — not the court's and not the clerk's.

11. **Get a certified copy of the expungement order** from the clerk.
12. **Send the Department of Justice request** with the court order. In Montana, sending the order to the arresting agency, the prosecutor, the clerk, and the Department of Justice, along with fingerprints and the Department of Justice form, is the petitioner's own responsibility. Allow about 30 days for processing. There is no fee for the request.

If the court **resentences** you instead, there is no Department of Justice stage. The conviction stands with a changed sentence, so there is nothing for the Department of Justice to delete.

## Words that are not interchangeable

- **Expungement** in Montana means permanently destroying, deleting, or erasing a record from the state criminal history system the Department of Justice keeps.
- What happens at the courts, police agencies, and prosecutors' offices is **sealing** — not destruction.
- **Removal** is the Department of Justice's word for deleting a record of something that was never a conviction.
- **Resentencing** and **redesignation** are different outcomes again, and neither one is expungement or sealing.

## What this does not do

- It does not promise a record will be erased, that nobody will ever see it, or that you qualify.
- It does not promise Montana relief will reach federal or FBI records, Tribal or out-of-state convictions, copies already held by private background-check companies, firearm rights, immigration consequences, or professional licensing.
- It says nothing about whether you may deny the conviction afterward. That is a question for a lawyer.

## Stop and get a lawyer if

- You are currently incarcerated. That alone is a reason to talk to a lawyer about this petition.
- You are not sure whether what you were convicted of is now allowed, or would now carry a lesser sentence.
- Your offence is not on the list the form gives you.
- The county attorney opposes the petition, or you want to ask for a hearing.
- You are not a U.S. citizen.
- You are weighing expungement against resentencing and the difference matters to you.
