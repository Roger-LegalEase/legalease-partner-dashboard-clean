# Clearing an older Washington conviction after probation — what you need to do

This guide explains how to complete, sign and file your motion to vacate an older
Washington conviction. Read it before you sign anything.

**This is not an official court form, and it is not legal advice.** A Washington
superior court decides whether your conviction is vacated, and it does not have
to grant it. Nothing here guarantees an outcome.

## First: is this the right law for your case?

This route is for **older convictions — offences committed before 1 July 1984**.

If your offence was committed on or after 1 July 1984, this is the wrong law.
Those cases run under different statutes, and filing this document will not help
you. Check the offence date before you go any further.

## Which of the two paths applies to you?

The statute has two separate paths with two very different timing rules. They are
easy to mix up.

**Path one — withdraw your plea or set aside the verdict, then dismiss.** This is
open to you if you either completed the conditions of probation for the whole
period, or were discharged from probation early. There is no waiting period, but
there *is* a deadline: you must apply **before the maximum period of punishment
for the offence expires**. If that has passed, this path is closed.

**Path two — vacate the record.** This one becomes available only **after your
period of probation has expired**. The court then applies a set of tests, as those
tests would apply to someone convicted before 1 July 1984.

If you are not sure which path fits, talk to an attorney before filing. Choosing
the wrong one wastes the filing.

## What this does and does not do

The court **may** grant this relief — both paths are discretionary. The court is
not required to say yes.

And this is important: even if you win, **the conviction can still be used against
you in a later criminal case**. The statute keeps it available for pleading and
proof in any later prosecution for a different offence, exactly as if you had
never been given probation. This relief is not erasure.

## Step 1 — Check your court's local rules

Washington courts warn that some counties add their own local requirements. We
could not establish which counties do this or what they require, so we have not
guessed. Call the clerk of the sentencing court and ask what that court requires
for a motion to vacate.

## Step 2 — Check the details, then sign by hand

Read every line. Confirm your name, any other names you have used, and the case
details against your own court records. Lines marked "to be confirmed" are values
we will not guess — the case number, for example. Fill those in from your records
or ask the court clerk. Then sign and date by hand.

## Step 3 — What you write yourself

The statement of the circumstances you are relying on is **yours to write**. We do
not write it for you, and nobody else may write it for you.

## Step 4 — File with the sentencing court

File the motion and the proposed order with the clerk of the court that sentenced
you. On the vacation path that is the superior court that imposed the sentence.

**About serving the prosecutor:** the statute does not say you have to serve
anyone, and no state form tells us otherwise. Rather than invent a step, we have
left it out — so ask the clerk whether that court's local rules require you to
give the prosecuting attorney a copy. Do not assume either way.

**About the fee:** we cannot tell you what this costs. Our source does not
establish a filing fee, so no amount appears anywhere in your documents. Ask the
clerk, and do not pay on the strength of a number you did not get from the court.

## Step 5 — What happens afterwards

If the court grants the order, the clerk sends it to the Washington State Patrol
identification section and to any local police agency, those records are updated,
and the information goes to the FBI. After that, the record cannot be given out
except to criminal justice enforcement agencies.

Those are the clerk's and the agencies' duties, not yours. Your documents do not
claim any of it has already happened.

## When to stop and get a lawyer

- Your offence was committed on or after 1 July 1984.
- You are not sure whether the maximum period of punishment has expired.
- You did not complete probation and were not discharged early.
- You are not sure which of the two paths applies to you.
- You need the conviction gone for immigration, firearms, or professional
  licensing purposes — this relief is narrower than people expect.
