# How to complete and file your Ohio application to seal a conviction or bail-forfeiture record

This packet asks the court that sentenced you (or, for an out-of-state or federal conviction, an Ohio court of common pleas) to seal the record of a conviction or a misdemeanor bail forfeiture under Ohio law.

These instructions are for you only. Do not file this page with the court.

## Before you start

- Get your complete Ohio criminal history from BCI and the docket from every court involved. Confirm the exact charge, offense degree, disposition, final discharge date, and whether any charges are pending.
- Your waiting period runs from final discharge — when your sentence, parole, all fines and all restitution are complete — not from the conviction date. Unpaid court costs are not part of the sentence and do not delay eligibility.
- If one incident produced several charges and any of them ended differently, you generally cannot seal only part of that record. Collect every charge and every disposition first.

## Get the court's own application

Ohio has no single statewide form. Each court maintains its own application, and some courts use different packets for convictions and non-convictions. Ask the clerk of the sentencing court for that court's application, then copy the drafted content from this packet onto the court's own paperwork. Where this packet shows a bracketed item such as the docket number or the court's name, copy the correct value from your court records or confirm it with the clerk.

## Sign and file

1. Sign the application yourself. Ask the clerk whether your court requires the signature to be notarized.
2. File with the clerk of the sentencing court. The application fee is fifty dollars unless you qualify as indigent, and the court may add a local fee of up to fifty dollars. If you cannot afford the fee, ask the clerk for the court's indigency process.

## The hearing

- The court schedules a hearing 45 to 90 days after filing. The prosecutor is notified at least 60 days before the hearing and may object at least 30 days before it; victims may be heard where the law provides. You attend the hearing.
- Sealing is discretionary: the court weighs your interest in sealing against the government's legitimate needs, and considers rehabilitation and any objections.

## After an order

- The court forwards the order and your fingerprint record to the state Bureau of Criminal Identification and Investigation (BCI), or orders fingerprinting. The Ohio Attorney General also requires that signed orders be sent to BCI with the Attorney General's own request form — that official form is not included in this packet; get it from the Ohio Attorney General or ask the clerk.
- Know what sealing does: the record becomes private from most employers, landlords, schools and the general public, but it still exists, and certain employers, licensing boards, prosecutors, judges and police can still see it. BCI keeps a limited record where the statute requires. Do not expect the record to be completely gone from everywhere.
- If the prosecutor objects, if you have charges pending, if your record includes an excluded offense (for example traffic-chapter offenses, a felony offense of violence, an offense against a victim under 13, theft in office, a first- or second-degree felony, or first- or second-degree misdemeanor domestic violence), or if anything above does not match your situation — stop and talk to a lawyer or legal aid before going further.
